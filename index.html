The Crossing6 October 1973

At 2:00 PM on the tenth of Ramadan, the Egyptian army crossed the Suez Canal, broke through the Bar Lev Line and restored the pride of a nation.

Read the story
The road to war

From the 1967 setback to the crossing

June 1967

The setback

In six days in June 1967, Israel took the Sinai Peninsula, Gaza, the West Bank and the Golan Heights. Egypt lost most of its air force and the Suez Canal became a front line. Recovering the land became a national mission.

1968–1970

The War of Attrition

Egypt rebuilt its army and fought a long war of artillery duels, commando raids and air battles along the canal. On 9 March 1969 Chief of Staff Abdel Moneim Riad was killed at the front, and the date is now Martyrs’ Day. A ceasefire in August 1970 stopped the shelling but not the occupation.

1970

The missile wall

Egypt rebuilt its air defenses with Soviet help, packing the canal zone with surface-to-air missile batteries. Under Mohamed Ali Fahmy, the Air Defense Forces made deep Israeli air raids costly and later gave the crossing army an umbrella to fight under.

1970–1972

A new president, a hard choice

Gamal Abdel Nasser died on 28 September 1970 and Anwar Sadat succeeded him. Sadat tried diplomacy first, offering a phased withdrawal and the reopening of the canal in 1971, which went nowhere. In July 1972 he sent Soviet military advisers home, and by then he had concluded that only force would break the stalemate.

1969–1973

The Bar Lev Line

Israel lined the canal’s east bank with a sand rampart 18 to 25 meters high, reinforced with concrete and running about 160 kilometers. Behind it stood 22 forts and 35 strongpoints. Israeli planners expected a breach to take 24 to 48 hours, and many called the line impregnable.

1971–1973

Secret planning, careful deception

The plan, first called High Minarets, became Operation Badr. Egypt agreed with Syria to strike together, rehearsed the crossing again and again, and hid its intentions: routine exercises, reservists announced as released, officers invited to apply for the Umrah pilgrimage, and rumors that President Sadat was ill. Troops learned the date only six hours before.

6–25 October 1973

The war, day by day

6 Oct, 14:00

Zero hour

More than 200 Egyptian aircraft strike airbases, missile batteries, command posts and radar stations across Sinai. At 14:05 nearly 2,000 guns open a 53-minute barrage on the Bar Lev Line. On the Golan Heights, Syria attacks at the same hour.

6 Oct, 14:20

Allahu Akbar

The first wave of 4,000 infantrymen paddles across the canal in about 2,500 boats, chanting “Allahu Akbar.” At 14:35 the Egyptian flag is raised on the east bank. At 15:30 the first strongpoint of the Bar Lev Line falls.

6 Oct, evening

The sand wall falls

Engineers carry water pumps across in rubber boats and wash passages through the rampart; the first opens in under an hour. By 17:30 twelve waves have put 32,000 men on the east bank. Between 22:30 and 01:30 the bridges go up, and tanks and vehicles cross all night.

7 Oct

Holding the bridgeheads

By dawn about 50,000 men and 400 tanks hold five bridgeheads, 6 to 9 kilometers deep. Commando groups delay Israeli reserves on the roads, and in El-Qantarah the 18th Division clears the town building by building. Planners had feared thousands of casualties; first-day losses were far lower.

8 Oct

The counterattack breaks

Israeli armor strikes the Second Army’s bridgehead near Ismailia. The 2nd Division lets it advance into a prepared killing zone, and in about 13 minutes more than 50 tanks are destroyed. Battalion commander Lt. Col. Asaf Yaguri is captured. By evening Egypt holds a strip about 15 kilometers deep, with 90,000 men and 980 tanks.

9–13 Oct

The pause

Israel turns its main effort to Syria and the Sinai front settles. Egypt’s navy keeps its blockade of the Bab el-Mandeb strait, closing the Red Sea to Israeli shipping. Moscow and Washington move to resupply their partners by air, and Syria presses Egypt to attack eastward to ease its position.

14 Oct

Armor and air battles

Egypt pushes eastward toward the Mitla and Gidi passes. About 1,000 Egyptian tanks meet some 800 Israeli tanks in one of the largest tank battles since World War II, and the advance is halted. Over the Delta, Egyptian MiG-21s fight Israeli aircraft in the Air Battle of Mansoura, remembered each year as Air Force Day.

15–17 Oct

Fighting at the Deversoir gap

On the night of 15–16 October Israeli forces cross the canal north of the Great Bitter Lake and begin to widen a corridor. Egyptian infantry and armor fight hard battles around the “Chinese Farm” and Ismailia to contain it, in some of the fiercest fighting of the war.

17–20 Oct

The oil weapon

Arab oil ministers announce production cuts, and Saudi Arabia, Libya and other producers impose an embargo on countries seen as backing Israel. Oil prices surge and the war becomes a global economic event.

22–25 Oct

Ceasefire

The UN Security Council calls for a ceasefire on 22 October. Fighting continues for days, and on 24–25 October soldiers and civilians in the city of Suez turn back an Israeli attempt to take it. The guns fall silent on 25 October, with Egyptian forces still holding ground on the east bank. The Third Army, cut off there, receives supplies under a UN arrangement.

The men behind the crossing

Key figures of the October War

Anwar Sadat

President and Supreme Commander, 1918–1981

Succeeded Nasser in 1970 and took the decision to go to war. He asked his generals to plan within Egypt’s means: cross the canal and hold a foothold in Sinai that would change the political picture. After the war he turned to diplomacy, and the Sinai came back through negotiation.

Gamal Abdel Nasser

President, 1918–1970

Rebuilt the army after 1967 on the principle that what was taken by force can only be recovered by force. He launched the War of Attrition and the missile wall, and died in September 1970, three years before the victory he had prepared.

Ahmed Ismail Ali

War Minister and Commander-in-Chief, 1917–1974

Appointed in October 1972, he backed a limited offensive and oversaw the planning of Operation Badr and its coordination with Syria. He was promoted to Field Marshal and died of cancer in December 1974.

Saad El-Shazly

Chief of Staff, 1922–2011

Chief of Staff from 1971 to December 1973. He argued for a limited war fought under the missile umbrella and helped shape the crossing plan. His memoir, The Crossing of the Suez, remains a key account of the war.

Mohamed Abdel Ghani El-Gamasy

Chief of Operations, 1921–2003

As head of the Operations Authority he turned the plan into detailed orders for the armed forces. He later became Chief of Staff and War Minister, and led Egypt’s military delegation to the Kilometer 101 talks after the ceasefire.

Hosni Mubarak

Air Force Commander

Commanded the Egyptian Air Force, whose opening strike of more than 200 aircraft hit Israeli airbases, missile batteries and radar stations. He later served as President of Egypt from 1981 to 2011.

Mohamed Ali Fahmy

Commander of Air Defense Forces

Called the father of Egyptian air defense. He built the missile wall that blunted Israeli air power over the canal zone and covered the crossing forces with an umbrella of missiles and guns.

Baqi Zaki Youssef

Military engineer, 1931–2019

Proposed using high-pressure water pumps to wash away the sand rampart, an idea drawn from his work on the Aswan High Dam. The method cut the time to open a passage from five or six hours to about two, with no explosives or heavy machinery.

Courage under fire

Heroic acts of the October War

The tank hunters

Small infantry teams with Sagger missiles and RPGs ambushed Israeli armor from the sand ramparts and ditches. Conscripts such as Mohamed Abdel Aty, credited with 23 tanks, and Mohamed El-Masry, credited with 27, including the tank of Lt. Col. Asaf Yaguri, became national heroes and were decorated for their bravery.

The Nubian code talkers

When Egypt’s radio codes were being broken, Sergeant Ahmed Idris suggested a code Israeli listeners could not understand: the Nubian language, which has no written alphabet. Nubian soldiers were trained to send commanders’ messages over the radio. The secret stayed classified for decades, and Idris received the Military Star in 2017.

Engineers against the sand wall

Egyptian engineers crossed in the first minutes, carrying pumps in rubber boats, and cut about 60 passages through a rampart Israeli planners believed would hold for a day or two. Bridge builders then assembled heavy and light bridges and ferries under fire, moving them between passages to dodge air strikes.

The frogmen

On the night of 5 October, Egyptian engineers and frogmen blocked the underwater pipes on the east bank that were meant to pour burning oil onto the canal. Naval frogmen also raided the Bala’eem oil installations in the Gulf of Suez, disabling a huge drilling rig.

The Sa’iqa commandos

Sa’iqa, meaning lightning, commandos were landed by helicopter deep in Sinai and on the coastal road to delay Israeli reserves. On 7 October one company ambushed an armored brigade near Romani and closed the road for hours. The units paid a heavy price, but they slowed reinforcements and won praise even from an Israeli divisional commander.

Closing the Bab el-Mandeb

Egyptian submarines, destroyers and missile boats sailed days earlier with sealed orders, opened on the morning of 6 October. Within hours Israeli shipping to Eilat had stopped, a blockade 2,500 kilometers from Israel that its navy and air force could not lift.

Every branch, one mission

The forces of the crossing

Infantry

The first wave

Four thousand men crossed in the first minutes and 32,000 in about three hours, carrying anti-tank missiles and RPGs on their backs and on 2,200 hand carts. They formed the bridgeheads and held them until armor arrived.

Engineers

Masters of the canal

They cut about 60 passages through the sand rampart, cleared minefields, and built heavy bridges, light bridges and ferries. The speed of the whole operation depended on how fast they worked.

Air Force

The opening blow

More than 200 aircraft hit airbases, missile batteries, command posts and radar stations in the first minutes, and the Air Force fought on through the war. Egypt marks Air Force Day on 14 October, the date of the Air Battle of Mansoura.

Air Defense

The missile umbrella

Layers of SA-2, SA-3 and SA-6 missiles, radar-guided anti-aircraft guns and shoulder-fired SA-7s sheltered the canal zone, which limited the effect of Israeli air strikes on the bridgeheads.

Navy

Guardians of the sea

Submarines and destroyers closed the Bab el-Mandeb strait to Israeli shipping. Missile boats and coastal artillery shelled targets on the Mediterranean and Red Sea coasts, while frogmen raided oil installations.

Artillery

The thunder

Nearly 2,000 guns, howitzers and mortars fired a 53-minute barrage, one of the largest in history, with an estimated 10,500 shells in the first minute. It silenced the Bar Lev Line’s artillery and shielded the first boats.

Decisive battles

Where the war was fought

6Oct

Breaking the Bar Lev Line

Strongpoint by strongpoint, Egyptian infantry isolated and stormed the forts along the canal while engineers opened the rampart. Most were encircled or captured within days. Only Fort Budapest, at the northern end, stayed in Israeli hands and held out against later Egyptian attacks.

7Oct

El-Qantarah

Troops of the 18th Infantry Division cleared the town building by building in close combat. By the end of 7 October the town and two nearby forts were under Egyptian control.

8Oct

Ismailia: the killing zone

Brig. Gen. Hassan Abu Sa’ada, commanding the 2nd Infantry Division, guessed where Israeli armor would strike and drew it into a prepared killing zone. Tanks, missile teams and tank hunters opened fire from three sides, and more than 50 tanks were destroyed in about 13 minutes.

14Oct

The push toward the passes

To ease pressure on Syria, Egyptian armored divisions advanced beyond the missile umbrella toward the Mitla and Gidi passes. Facing prepared Israeli armor and air power, the attack was halted with heavy losses, and Egyptian forces returned to the defensive.

15–18Oct

Ismailia and the Chinese Farm

After Israeli forces crossed at Deversoir, Egyptian infantry and armor fought to hold the ground around Ismailia and the Chinese Farm. The fighting, among irrigation ditches in dust and darkness, was among the bloodiest of the war for both sides.

24Oct

The defense of Suez

Israeli forces pushed into the city of Suez on 24 October. Army units, police and townspeople fought in the streets and turned the attack back. The city stayed in Egyptian hands, and the ceasefire took hold the next day.

Operation Badr

How the crossing worked

Operation Badr takes its name from the Battle of Badr, the first Muslim victory, fought in Ramadan in 624. The date, 6 October, was chosen with care: the canal’s current and tide were right, a full moon would light the bridge-building, and on Yom Kippur Israeli roads stood empty and much of its army was on leave.

For months Egypt masked its preparations. Troop movements were presented as exercises Israel had grown used to, reservists were publicly released, officers were invited to apply for the Umrah pilgrimage, and rumors spread that President Sadat was ill. Israel’s warning came only about nine and a half hours before the attack, far short of the day or two it expected.

By the end of 8 October five Egyptian divisions held a strip about 15 kilometers deep along the whole east bank, protected by the missile umbrella. It was the first time since 1967 that Egyptian troops held ground in Sinai, and it changed the political calculation for everyone involved.

Beyond the battlefield

A war fought on many fronts

Golan

The Syrian front

At the same hour as the crossing, Syrian forces attacked on the Golan Heights. The two presidents had agreed on the war together, and their generals met secretly in Alexandria in August 1973. Syria made early gains, but Israeli reserves stopped its advance within days.

Allies

Arab solidarity

Several Arab states sent troops, aircraft, money or supplies, and Arab leaders stood together politically. Egypt’s diplomacy had been preparing that support for months.

Oil

The oil weapon

In mid-October Arab producers cut output and embargoed countries backing Israel. Prices surged, Western governments took notice, and for the first time oil became a political weapon on a global scale.

Home

The home front

Egyptians of every background rallied behind the army. Volunteers, doctors and nurses staffed field hospitals, communities donated blood, and families waited for news from the front.

Superpowers

The superpowers

Moscow resupplied Egypt and Syria, and from 14 October Washington airlifted weapons to Israel in Operation Nickel Grass. The war brought the two powers close to confrontation, and they jointly sponsored the UN ceasefire resolution.

Israel

The shock of surprise

In Israel the war was a national shock. The Agranat Commission investigated why the attack was not anticipated, and Prime Minister Golda Meir resigned in April 1974. The belief in the Bar Lev Line and in Israeli intelligence had collapsed.

Keeping the memory alive

The 6th of October Panorama

In Heliopolis, Cairo, the Panorama museum and memorial opened on 5 October 1989. Visitors stand on a rotating platform inside an enormous circular painting that recreates the crossing and the battles that followed, surrounded by models, weapons, and sound and light effects.

Built with the help of North Korean artists and engineers, it stands on about 32,000 square meters. Outside, tanks and guns from the war are on display, along with a statue of soldiers crossing the canal.

The legacy

From the battlefield to peace

Heroes of the crossing

A statue of Egyptian soldiers crossing the canal, outside the Panorama in Cairo

The crossing broke the “no war, no peace” stalemate. It opened the way to the disengagement agreements of 1974, the Camp David Accords of 1978 and the 1979 peace treaty, and Sinai returned to Egypt in stages, with the final Israeli withdrawal on 25 April 1982. Taba followed in 1989.
